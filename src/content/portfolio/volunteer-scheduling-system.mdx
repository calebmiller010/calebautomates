A church's volunteer teams ran on a group text. One person built each rotation by hand, kept
track of who was out of town, and — every time someone couldn't make it — chased replies until
somebody agreed to cover. It worked, as long as that one person had the time. When they didn't,
Sunday got uncomfortable.

## What I built

A scheduling system that does the coordinator's job quietly in the background:

1. **Rotations generate themselves, fairly.** The system balances how often each person serves,
   works around the dates they've said they're away, and eases people back in after time off —
   instead of someone juggling it on paper.
2. **Volunteers handle their own changes.** Each person gets a private link — no account, no
   password. From it they see their dates, mark time away, and swap or request cover without
   anyone in the middle.
3. **The schedule goes where people already look.** It shows up in their phone's calendar,
   prints as a clean one-page PDF, and can send optional reminders.
4. **Each team runs its own way.** One team rotates individuals; another rotates whole groups.
   Each team's leader manages their own team, and each team keeps its own off-weeks.

## The design principle

These are people who already know each other and already have each other's phone numbers. The
system isn't a social app and doesn't try to be — its job is to handle the logistics and stay
out of the way. That's the bar for any internal tool: people should notice it only when it saves
them a conversation.

## Why this matters for a business like yours

Swap "volunteers" for "crew," "techs," or "front-desk staff" and this is one of the most common
back-office headaches there is: shift coverage, on-call rotations, crew assignments, who's out
next week. When it lives in a group text and one manager's head, it's fragile — and it's exactly
the kind of thing a business can't hand off. A system like this turns it into something that runs
itself.
