Most "is your business ready for automation" checklists are lead-gen bait dressed up as advice. They ask you ten leading questions, all roads point to "yes, buy now," and there's a fake score at the end that always says you're leaving money on the table.

This isn't that. Some of the answers below are going to be "no, don't automate that yet." I'd rather tell you that now than sell you something that sits unused in three months.

Go through the list. Be honest. At the end, you'll have a real sense of where the low-hanging fruit actually is — and whether it's worth picking right now.

## How to use this

For each question, answer yes or no based on what's actually happening today, not what you wish were happening. Don't overthink it — if you have to argue yourself into a "yes," it's probably a "no."

Count your yeses at the end. More yeses means more places where a small, well-scoped fix would save real time. It's not a score, it's a map.

## Document handling

**1. Do you or someone on your team retype information from one document into another system?**
Invoices into accounting software. Applications into a spreadsheet. Contract terms into a tracker. If a human is reading a PDF and typing what they see somewhere else, that's a pattern automation handles well — extract, map, insert.

**2. Do documents pile up waiting for someone to "get to them"?**
A shared drive folder of unprocessed intake forms, a stack of scanned receipts, an inbox of attachments nobody's opened yet. Backlog is usually a sign the manual step is the bottleneck, not the people.

**3. Is the same document type (invoice, order, application, report) processed the same way every time?**
This matters more than volume. If every invoice needs a judgment call, automation will fight you. If 90% of them follow the same shape, that 90% can run itself.

## Client communications

**4. Do you send the same email or message, lightly edited, more than a few times a week?**
Onboarding instructions, appointment confirmations, status updates, "here's what happens next." If you're rewriting the same paragraph from memory, it should be a template with a trigger, not a task on your list.

**5. Do clients ask "what's the status of my thing?" and someone has to go check and reply?**
That's a signal there's no automatic notification where one would clearly help. The check-and-reply loop is exactly the kind of task that disappears with a simple trigger.

**6. Would a client notice — in a good way — if they got a confirmation or update instantly instead of hours later?**
If yes, you have a responsiveness gap that's currently being filled by a person's attention, which is your most expensive and most limited resource.

## Reporting

**7. Does anyone spend time each week (or month) pulling numbers from two or more places into one summary?**
Combining a sales report and a scheduling tool into one weekly update. Pulling from your CRM and your bookkeeping software to see the same picture. Manual aggregation is one of the most reliable places to find hours back.

**8. Do you find out about a problem (low inventory, missed follow-up, overdue invoice) later than you'd like?**
If the information already exists in a system somewhere but nobody's watching it in real time, that's not a people problem, it's a missing alert.

## Scheduling and coordination

**9. Does booking, confirming, or rescheduling appointments involve back-and-forth messages?**
Phone tag and email tag to land on a time is something most scheduling tools handle on their own today. If you're still doing it by hand, it's usually one of the easier wins on this list.

**10. Does someone manually remind people (staff or clients) about upcoming things — shifts, appointments, deadlines?**
Manual reminders get forgotten under a busy week, which is exactly when they matter most. That inconsistency is the tell.

## Data entry and internal handoffs

**11. Does work sit "waiting for someone to notice it" between two steps in a process?**
A completed form that someone has to remember to check. A finished task that has to be manually flagged for the next person. Gaps between steps, where nothing happens until a human notices, are usually fixable with a simple handoff trigger.

**12. If the person who currently does a manual step were out for two weeks, would that step just... not happen?**
This is the sharpest question on the list. If a process depends on one person's memory and habits rather than a system, it's fragile — and fragility is a cost even when nothing's going wrong.

## Scoring yourself, honestly

**More yeses means more low-hanging fruit.** Not more urgency, more targets. Each "yes" above points to the same underlying fix pattern: something a person currently does by re-reading, re-typing, checking, or reminding can instead be triggered automatically and handled without someone having to remember to do it. The specifics differ, but the shape repeats.

A handful of yeses, especially in document handling and reporting, usually means there's a real week of hours to get back without touching anything client-facing. That's normally the safest place to start.

## When automation is honestly not worth it yet

I'd be doing you a disservice if I skipped this part.

**If your volume is genuinely low.** Say, as a made-up example, five invoices a month versus fifty a week — that's illustrative, not a rule, but the idea holds: if the volume is small, the time saved won't cover the setup. Do the math before you do the project.

**If the process is still changing.** If you changed your intake form three times this quarter because you're still figuring out what you need, automate later. Automating a moving target means rebuilding it every time the target moves.

**If nobody's actually sure what the process is.** If you'd get three different answers from three team members about how something gets done, that's a process problem to solve first. Automating chaos just makes chaos happen faster.

**If it's a one-time thing.** Automation pays for itself through repetition. A task you do once, or rarely, usually isn't worth the build.

None of this is automation being oversold to you. It's just where the math doesn't work yet. It might work later, once volume or process settles — but "later" isn't "now," and I'd rather say that up front than take on work that doesn't pay for itself.
